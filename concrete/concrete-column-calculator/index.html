Quick answer

How many bags of concrete for a 12-inch sonotube?

A 12-inch sonotube poured 4 ft deep holds 3.14 ft³ of concrete, which is six 80 lb bags (0.60 ft³ each) with a 5% tube-form waste allowance. Volume grows with depth and with the square of the diameter, so a 16-inch tube of the same depth takes about 10 bags.

Rule of thumb:
A 12-inch tube holds about 0.79 ft³ of concrete per foot of height.
Formula:
V = (π × d² ÷ 4) × h

Round vs square: the volume formulas

A round column holds about 21.5% less concrete than a square one of the same width, because a circle fills π/4 of its bounding square.

Use the tube's inside diameter: a "12-inch" sonotube is 12 in inside. For belled piers (widened base for uplift or bearing), add a truncated cone: roughly one-third the bell height times the sum of the top area, bottom area and their geometric mean. In practice, adding one extra 80 lb bag per bell covers it.

Placement tips that prevent honeycombing

The 4-point pier checklist

Brace every tube plumb in two directions before any concrete arrives. Fill in one continuous lift, rodding every 2 ft. Set post bases or anchor bolts within 15 minutes of topping off. Keep tubes damp-cured and braced for 48 hours before loading. Full strength timelines are in the curing guide.

Ordering for scattered piers

A pump truck or a power buggy beats wheelbarrowing ready-mix across a site with many piers, because concrete stiffening in a wheelbarrow queue is how the last three piers end up honeycombed. For bag mixing, one mixer and a two-person crew place about 10–12 bags an hour; plan crew size against the 90-minute working window.

Frequently asked questions

How many bags of concrete fill a 12-inch sonotube?
Six 80 lb bags for a typical 4 ft deep pier. A 12 in tube holds 0.785 ft³ per foot of height, so a 4 ft pier holds 3.14 ft³: six 80 lb bags (0.60 ft³ each), rounded up with a little waste. The table on this page lists per-foot volumes for every standard tube size.
Do round or square columns use less concrete?
Round, every time. A circle covers π/4 ≈ 78.5% of the square that contains it, so a 12 in round column uses 21.5% less concrete than a 12 in square one. That's why cardboard tube forms dominate pier work: less material and no formwork carpentry.
Should deck piers be poured in one lift?
Yes. Each pier is small enough to fill in one continuous placement. Rod or vibrate in 2 ft increments as you fill to release trapped air, and set your post anchor while the concrete is plastic. A cold joint mid-pier is a structural defect in a column.
How much rebar goes in a concrete column?
At least 1% of the cross-section for structural columns: minimum 1% longitudinal steel (4 bars minimum for square/round ties) with ties per ACI 318 §10.7 or IS 456 §26.5.3. Simple deck piers under light loads often use a single centered #4 or the anchor bolt alone. Follow your approved plan, not a website.
Can I mix bagged concrete for columns?
Yes, and for a handful of piers it is often the better choice. Piers are the one job where bags genuinely compete with ready-mix: each pier needs only a few cubic feet, pours are spread across the site, and a truck's short-load fee outweighs bag premiums for small counts. Above roughly 15–20 piers, price both options.
How do you calculate concrete for a column?
Multiply the cross-section area by the height. For a round column that is π × diameter² ÷ 4 × height; for a square one it is side² × height. Multiply by the number of columns, add about 5% waste for tube forms, and divide cubic feet by 27 for cubic yards.

Piers plus a slab or footing?

Run each element through its calculator, then combine the order. One truck is cheaper than two.
