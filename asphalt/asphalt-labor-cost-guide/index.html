Asphalt labor costs: crews and productivity

Labor is 20–30% of an installed asphalt price, and it is bought by the crew-day, not the hour. Understanding how a crew is built and paced explains most of what looks odd in paving bids.

Quick answer

How much does asphalt paving labor cost?

Asphalt paving labor is typically 20–30% of the installed price on residential work, or about $0.60–1.50 per square foot for a new 3-inch driveway. A 5–8 person paving crew costs the contractor roughly $300–450 per hour loaded, and small jobs are billed by the crew-day because setup and cleanup take most of it.

Rule of thumb:
Material 40–50%, labor 20–30%, equipment and trucking most of the rest.

Who is on a paving crew?

A residential paving crew is five to eight people, with loaded rates from $35 to $75 per hour by role.

Productivity: tons per crew-day

Residential crews place 100–300 tons a day and highway crews 500–1,500, and productivity is the lever that converts hourly rates into unit costs. A highway crew running continuous truck cycles places 500–1,500 tons per day, so even a $450-per-hour crew adds only $2–4 per ton of labor. A residential crew places 100–300 tons per day across one or several stops, and on a single small driveway the effective figure can drop below 30 tons: the same crew, the same hourly cost, spread across a fraction of the output. That ratio, not wage rates, is why patch work costs $3 per square foot in labor while lot paving costs $0.50.

The paver sets the pace, so staff to it

Paying for a bigger crew is often cheaper per square foot. Every paver stop risks a cold joint and roller marks, and short-handed crews stop the machine constantly. If a contractor proposes a six-person crew over a four-person crew for the same bid price, that is a mark in their favor, not padding.

Frequently asked questions

What share of an asphalt quote is labor?
Typically 20–30% of installed cost on residential work. Material runs 40–50%, equipment and trucking take most of the rest. On small jobs the labor share rises because the crew spends proportionally more time on setup, hand work and cleanup relative to tons placed.
How big is a typical paving crew?
Five to eight people: a foreman, a paver operator, a screed operator, one or two roller operators, and two or three laborers on rakes and lutes handling edges, transitions and hand work around structures. Highway crews run larger with dedicated truck spotters and traffic control; a small patch crew may be three.
How many tons does a crew place per day?
A highway crew with continuous truck cycles places 500–1,500 tons a day. Residential crews place 100–300, not because they work slower but because driveways involve moves between jobs, hand-raked edges and small tonnage per stop. The paver itself is rarely the constraint; truck timing and hand work are.
What do paving crew members earn?
About $35–75 per hour loaded (wage plus payroll taxes, insurance and benefits) in 2026 depending on role and market. Operators sit at the top, laborers at the bottom, and union metros push the whole band higher. A full residential crew therefore costs the contractor roughly $300–450 per hour on the clock.
Can I save money by helping the crew?
No. No reputable contractor will let you work inside an active paving operation, because 300°F material and moving rollers make it an insurance non-starter. Where owners genuinely save on labor is before the crew arrives: demolition, clearing, and hauling where the contract allows it. Ask the estimator which prep items they will credit.
Why does a bigger crew not cost more per square foot?
Because the paver sets the pace. A short-handed crew makes the machine wait: rakers fall behind at edges, the screed stops, and stopped pavers leave roller marks and cold joints. An extra laborer at $45 per hour who keeps a $350-per-hour operation moving pays for himself several times over, which is why efficient contractors staff up rather than down.
