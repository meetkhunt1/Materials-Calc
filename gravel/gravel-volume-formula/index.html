The gravel volume formula

Length times width times depth: three numbers, one multiplication, and two unit conversions where nearly every estimating error hides. Here is the formula with every trap flagged.

Quick answer

How do you calculate gravel volume?

Gravel volume in cubic feet equals length × width × depth, with depth converted from inches to feet (inches ÷ 12). Divide cubic feet by 27 for cubic yards, then multiply by 1.42 for tons of common loose gravel. A 12 × 18 ft area at 3 inches deep is 54 ft³, or 2.0 yd³ and 2.84 tons.

Rule of thumb:
If the answer implies a dump truck for a garden path, you multiplied by inches instead of feet.
Formula:
Tons = L × W × (in ÷ 12) ÷ 27 × 1.42

The volume formula

This one line sizes every rectangular gravel job from a planter strip to a haul road. Everything that follows (yards, tons, cost) is unit conversion applied to its output. For non-rectangular sites, compute the area with the shape formulas in the measurement guide and multiply by depth exactly the same way.

The unit trap: inches are not feet

The 12× mistake

Multiplying feet by feet by inches gives a number twelve times too big. A 12 × 3 ft path at 3 inches deep is 12 × 3 × 0.25 = 9 ft³, but plugging in the raw 3 gives 108, which prices out as a 12-ton order for a 1-ton job. If your answer implies more than a dump truck for a garden path, you have almost certainly hit this trap.

Converting to cubic yards

From cubic yards to tons

The conversion runs both ways: tons ÷ 1.42 = loose cubic yards of common gravel, so a quote in the other unit is easy to check. The weight chart covers every direction of this conversion, and the coverage guide translates the result into square feet on the ground.

The metric shortcut

Use metric to audit your imperial math

The two systems make independent errors. Run a quick metric version of any large order (1 ft = 0.305 m). If the tonnes and tons figures don't land within about 10% of each other (a tonne is 2,205 lb vs 2,000), one of your conversions is wrong, and you've found it for free.

Formula questions

What is the formula for gravel volume?
Volume = length × width × depth, with all three dimensions in the same unit. In feet, the result is cubic feet; divide by 27 for cubic yards. The full imperial chain is: ft × ft × (inches ÷ 12) = ft³, then ÷ 27 = yd³, then × 1.42 = tons for common gravel.
Why do I divide depth in inches by 12?
To convert inches to feet, because the formula needs every dimension in the same unit, and depth is habitually measured in inches while length and width are in feet. Dividing by 12 turns 3 inches into 0.25 ft. Multiplying feet by feet by inches without converting inflates the answer twelvefold, the single most expensive mistake in DIY estimating.
Why divide cubic feet by 27 for cubic yards?
Because a cubic yard holds 27 cubic feet: it is a cube measuring 3 ft on each side, and 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. People sometimes divide by 9 (a square yard) or by 3 (a linear yard), and both are wrong by a large margin. If your cubic-yard figure isn't roughly 1/27th of your cubic-foot figure, recheck the division.
How do I convert gravel volume to tons?
Multiply volume by density, then convert to tons: cubic feet × density in lb/ft³ ÷ 2,000, or the shortcut cubic yards × 1.42 for common gravel (1.3 for pea gravel, 1.69 for loose crusher run). Density is the only step where gravel type matters; the volume math is identical for every material.
How much gravel do I need per square foot?
Depth in inches ÷ 12 cubic feet per square foot. At 2 inches that's 0.167 ft³ (about 17.5 lb of common gravel); at 3 inches, 0.25 ft³ (about 26 lb); at 4 inches, 0.333 ft³ (35 lb). Multiply by your square footage and you have total volume without ever writing the full formula.
Does the formula work for metric units?
Yes, and with fewer steps. Meters × meters × meters gives cubic meters directly; multiply by density in tonnes per cubic meter (1.68 for common gravel) and you have tonnes. There is no 12, no 27 and no 2,000 anywhere in the metric chain, which is why estimators who work in both systems prefer it.
Should I round up or add a percentage?
Both, in that order: add 10% for settling and edge waste (15% for compacted stone or rough subgrade), then round up to the supplier's ordering increment, usually a half ton or half yard. Running short costs a second delivery fee of $50–150, so the allowance is cheaper than the alternative.
