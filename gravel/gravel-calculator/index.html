Quick answer

How many tons of gravel do I need?

Multiply length × width × depth in feet, divide by 27 for cubic yards, then multiply by about 1.42 tons per cubic yard for loose common gravel. A 10×10 ft area at 3 inches deep needs 1.02 yd³, or about 1.44 tons including a 10% waste allowance. Crusher run bases run heavier, near 1.9 tons per yard.

Rule of thumb:
One ton of common gravel covers about 76 ft² at 3 in deep.
Formula:
Tons = Area × depth × density ÷ 2,000 × (1 + waste)

How do you calculate tons of gravel?

Tons of gravel equal area times depth in feet times the material's density, divided by 2,000 lb per ton, plus a waste allowance.

If you estimate more than one material, notice the pattern: the concrete calculator multiplies by 150 lb/ft³, the asphalt calculator by 145, and gravel by 96–140 depending on type. One method, three materials: the full walkthrough is in how to calculate gravel.

Ordering mistakes to avoid

Most short or over-sized gravel orders come from using the wrong density or skipping the settling allowance.

The four classic gravel errors

1. Using the 1.4 t/yd³ rule for crusher run: compacted base runs 1.9 and you'll arrive 25% short. 2. Skipping the settling allowance on soft subgrade, where the first lift presses into the soil. 3. Comparing a per-yard quote against a per-ton quote without converting. A "cheaper" yard price is often the more expensive material. 4. Ordering decorative stone by coverage numbers written for common gravel; lava rock covers double per ton, river rock about the same.

Round to the half ton

Quarries load in bucket increments anyway (~0.5–1 ton per bucket). Order in half-ton steps and tell the loader operator the total. Chasing 0.1-ton precision just annoys everyone at the scale house.

Frequently asked questions

How much gravel do I need for 100 square feet?
At a 3-inch depth: 100 × 0.25 = 25 ft³ = 0.93 yd³ ≈ 1.3 tons of common gravel. Halve it for 1.5 inches, double for 6. With a 10% allowance, order 1.5 tons.
How many tons is a cubic yard of gravel?
About 1.42 tons for loose common gravel (roughly 2,800 lb per cubic yard). Pea gravel runs lighter (~1.3), compacted crusher run heavier (~1.9). That spread is why the calculator asks for the type: density decides the answer.
Should I order gravel by the ton or by the yard?
Quarries scale-weigh trucks, so tons is the precise unit; landscape yards often sell loose cubic yards by the bucket. Get both numbers from the calculator and compare quotes in whichever unit each supplier uses. The conversion chart covers the math.
How deep should gravel be?
Walkways: 2–3 in over compacted base. Patios and pads: 3–4 in. Driveways: 8–12 in total in layers (see the driveway calculator). Drainage applications: whatever the pipe design calls for, typically 4–6 in around the pipe.
Does the calculator account for compaction?
Yes. The type dropdown handles it: loose materials use loose density, and crusher run uses its compacted density since bases are always compacted. For a detailed loose-vs-in-place breakdown, the crushed stone calculator reports both volumes.
How much gravel base do I need under a patio, shed or driveway?
Plan on 4 inches of compacted crusher run under patios and sheds and 6–8 inches under driveways. Pick crusher run in the calculator so it uses the compacted density (about 1.9 tons per cubic yard); a 10×12 ft shed pad at 4 inches needs about 3.1 tons with a 10% waste allowance.
Does this work as a landscape gravel calculator?
Yes. Choose pea gravel or river rock, enter the bed size and a 2–3 inch depth, and the calculator returns tons and cubic yards. Decorative beds over landscape fabric rarely need more than 3 inches; deeper stone just shifts underfoot.
How much does a truckload of gravel cover?
About 800 ft² at 4 inches deep. A 14-ton tandem load is about 10 loose cubic yards, which spreads to roughly 800 ft² at 4 inches or 1,600 ft² at 2 inches. One load handles most residential top-ups; new driveways typically need two to four.
